MPEG-G Microbiome Hackathon Series, 2025

Your microbes and your immune system are having a conversation. Eight teams built tools to listen in.

Two open competitions on Zindi asked data scientists to apply machine learning to microbiome data held in MPEG-G, the ISO/IEC 23092 standard for genomic information. Every winning method on this page is open source.

  • 1,200+ data scientists took part
  • 2 challenges
  • 8 winning solutions

A schematic of the problem, not a result. Some cytokines turned out to be predictable from microbial sequence alone and others, such as EGF and IL-22, did not. Working out which is which was one of the outcomes.

Two challenges, one dataset

Both competitions drew on a longitudinal multi-omic study by Zhou et al. (Cell Host & Microbe, 2024), which tracked microbiome composition, immune cytokines and clinical markers across four body sites in 86 participants over six years. This was the first time the dataset was released in MPEG-G format for open machine learning research.

Challenge 1, 20 June to 15 September 2025

Microbiome Classification

Can you tell where on the body a microbiome sample came from, using only its sequence profile?

Participants classified samples as stool, oral, skin or nasal from 16S rRNA profiles encoded in MPEG-G. Every team had to submit two models: one centralised, one trained by federated learning, where the model moves between data silos and the raw data never does. In clinical genomics, patient data frequently cannot cross a hospital or a border, so the question of whether federated models can match pooled ones is a practical one.

Challenge 1: the winning classifiers

All three top solutions misclassified between three and four of 1,068 held-out samples, and their federated variants stayed close to their centralised ones. Federated learning is often assumed to cost accuracy. Here it did not cost much.

First placeTeam Tiny Margins, Big Margins: Julius Mwangi (Juliuss), Kenya

Cyclic federated LightGBM

Partitioned the data by phenotype into four clients (control, crossover, diabetic, prediabetic) and passed a single LightGBM model around the ring, training locally at each stop before handing it on. One full circuit is one global round. No gradient averaging, no central aggregator.

  • Centralised

    LightGBM multiclass on pre-extracted numeric profile and metadata features, hyperparameters tuned with Optuna, 10-fold stratified cross-validation, one pooled model across all phenotypes and sites.

  • Federated

    The same LightGBM configuration per client, a fixed number of boosting rounds locally, then the updated model is passed to the next client. A global label encoder kept the target mapping consistent across silos.

Source: JuliusFx131/cyclic-federated-learning

Second placeTeam Kmers: Stephen Kolesh (Kenya) and Ronny Polle (Ghana)

Federated XGBoost over k-mer features

The strongest feature engineering of the three. Read counts, GC content, nucleotide fractions, Q20 and Q30 quality metrics, and k-mer frequencies for k of 2 to 5, built against canonical vocabularies fitted on the training set only. Splits were made with stratified group k-fold on subject ID, so no participant appeared on both sides of a fold.

  • Centralised

    XGBoost on the merged feature and metadata table, evaluated by multiclass log loss under stratified k-fold, with learning rate, tree depth and regularisation tuned for generalisation.

  • Federated

    A Flower pipeline in which each client ran 500 boosting rounds with a bagging strategy and sent trees to the server, aggregated into a global model by a custom strategy that persisted the model after every round.

Source: koleshjr/MPEG-G-Microbiome-Classification-Challenge

Third placeTeam Ever Learners: Duke Kongo (Ghana) and Nyashadzaishe Masvongo (Zimbabwe)

Stacked XGBoost and neural meta-learner

A two-stage stack. XGBoost generates out-of-fold class probabilities, and a multilayer perceptron is trained only on those probabilities rather than on the original features, which keeps the meta-learner small and makes the federated version cheap to communicate.

  • Centralised

    Stage one is a 5-fold XGBoost producing out-of-fold predictions and averaged test predictions. Stage two is an MLP of 1024, 512 and 256 units with batch normalisation, dropout at 0.2 and ReLU, trained with Adam and early stopping.

  • Federated

    Clients grouped by hashed subject ID train XGBoost locally, then train MLP meta-learners on their own out-of-fold outputs. Weights are combined on the server by federated averaging into one global meta-learner.

Source: codejoetheduke/3rd-place-solution-mpeg-g-classification

Challenge 2: five tracks, five findings

Each track attacked a different face of the host-microbiome relationship. The code for all five sits in this repository under solutions/challenge-2/.

Track 1Fares Mallouli, Tunisia

Predicting immune response from sequence

A k-mer counting, TF-IDF and LightGBM pipeline inside 5-fold cross-validation, predicting circulating cytokine levels from the microbial sequences found on a person's body.

The useful result was the failures. VCAM-1, PAI-1 and TNF-α were predicted well; EGF, IL-22, leptin and BDNF were not. That split the panel into microbiome-predictable and microbiome-independent cytokines, which tells later work where to look and where not to.

Track 2Chigozie Nkwocha, Nigeria

Which associations can be trusted

Rather than asking which microbe correlates with which cytokine, this entry asked which of those correlations survive resampling. A thousand bootstrap resamples with ridge regression produced confidence intervals, directional stability scores and coefficient-of-variation metrics for every k-mer and cytokine pair.

Resistin, IL-2, IFN-β and ICAM-1 held the most stable associations with microbial sequence. IL-27 and MIG were markedly less stable, suggesting their microbiome relationships are context dependent. Reliability mapping of this kind is a precondition for treating any microbiome association as a candidate biomarker.

Track 3Abdourahamane Idé Salifou, Niger, Carnegie Mellon University Africa

Interaction networks with graph neural networks

Bipartite graphs with taxa and cytokines as nodes and random forest feature importance as edges, encoded per body site by variational graph autoencoders, then run through pathway enrichment analysis.

Each site returned a distinct biological signature, discovered from the data rather than read from the literature:

  • StoolMetabolic pathways: butanoate metabolism, lipid processing
  • OralInflammatory signalling: JAK-STAT, NF-κB, IL-17
  • NasalAntiviral defence: type I interferon, RIG-I-like receptors
  • SkinTissue maintenance and growth factor activity

Track 4Team Ever Learners: Nyashadzaishe Masvongo and Duke Kongo

Latent health states

A variational autoencoder compressed 4,460 features, 66 cytokines and 4,394 bacterial taxa, into 64 dimensions, and the embeddings were then clustered. Eight states emerged:

  1. Acute inflammation. Extreme TNF-β with pathogenic Streptococcus, consistent with an active clinical event.
  2. Staphylococcal infection. S. aureus enrichment with a neutrophil chemokine signature.
  3. Metabolic inflammation. Raised leptin and GM-CSF, matching obesity-associated patterns.
  4. Metabolic dysbiosis. Hepatocyte growth factor elevation with dysbiotic communities.
  5. Healthy gut. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Eubacterium rectale, the butyrate-producing protective profile.
  6. Baseline healthy. Low cytokines, low pathogen abundance, and the largest cluster at 54% of samples.
  7. Immune modulated. A pattern suggesting immunosuppression.
  8. Chronic or exhausted. A pattern suggesting sustained immune fatigue.

The method depended on normalising cytokine and microbiome features separately before fusing them. Without that step the far more numerous microbiome features swamped the cytokine signal and everything collapsed into one uninformative cluster.

Track 5Team Ever Learners: Nyashadzaishe Masvongo and Duke Kongo

PyGenie, and a multimodal classifier

Two contributions. PyGenie is an open-source Python wrapper for the Genie MPEG-G decoder that streams straight from compressed format to model-ready features with no intermediate files, reported at five to ten times faster than conventional pipelines. Benchmarked over 2,901 microbiome files, the full pipeline finished in under two hours.

The second is a multimodal network that renders k-mer frequency distributions as images, encodes them with an EVA02-Large vision transformer, and fuses those features with taxa abundance and cytokine metadata.

88.4% accuracy on health status (healthy, insulin resistant, type 2 diabetes) and 99.5% on sample type, with the circular dependency between the two targets deliberately broken rather than left to inflate the numbers.

What came out of it

For microbiome science

Graph neural networks, variational autoencoders, multimodal transformers and federated learning pulled more out of this dataset than classical statistics alone. Body-site pathway signatures, latent health states, a stratified cytokine panel and a reliability map of associations are all usable results rather than demonstrations.

For genomic data standards

MPEG-G (ISO/IEC 23092) was built for compression and transport, and uptake in the machine learning community has been slow. These solutions worked directly against MPEG-G-encoded data, and PyGenie shows the decode step need not be a bottleneck.

For open science

Winners came from Kenya, Ghana, Zimbabwe, Nigeria, Niger and Tunisia, working alongside contributors at Stanford, Carnegie Mellon University Africa and European institutions. The code is open source, the data are CC-BY, and the results are reproducible.
